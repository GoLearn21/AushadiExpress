Field report · Portola / Tolan · compiled Aug 2026

The Tolan Playbook

A twelve-person team turned a cartoon alien into 100,000 paying subscribers and a seven-figure month in under a year. Here is the machine underneath it — and the parts of it that transfer to a test-prep app, a property-management app, and a pharmacy marketplace.

Emotional support and practical life management are not competing product features — they are the same feature. Ajay Mehta, co-founder & president, Portola

02 — Mechanism

Seven things that are actually doing the work

None of these is “we used a good LLM.” Every competitor has the same model access. These are product and go-to-market decisions, and each one is copyable.

The character is the advertisement

A cartoon alien is not a design flourish, it is the distribution strategy. It is customisable (skin, hair, outfit, voice), so it becomes yours — and a thing that is yours gets screenshotted and posted. Users share “my Tolan” the way they share a pet. The non-human form does double duty: it also suppresses romantic projection, which is what got every other companion app into trouble.

A shareable identity object turns every activated user into unpaid creative.

Memory is the aha, and it is measurable

The reported magic moment is precise: your Tolan brings up something you said yesterday, unprompted. That is the instant a tool becomes a relationship. Portola engineers memory as a retrieval system with compression and vector search, not a rolling transcript — each turn assembles a recent-conversation summary, a persona card, retrieved memories, and tone guidance.

Memory-recall misses −30%  /  next-day retention +20% when persona consistency improved.

Proactivity inverts who carries the conversation

ChatGPT waits for a prompt. A Tolan opens with something. It generates a set of daily activities, recommends a show, reacts to photos you upload, and asks about the thing you were stressed about last week. Users never face a blank box — which is the single biggest drop-off point in every chat product ever shipped.

The product opens the loop; the user only has to answer.

Emotional support and practical help are one feature

This is the load-bearing insight of the whole company. A user says she is stressed she hasn’t sorted out summer camp; the alien responds with empathy and a plan. Products that treat “wellness” and “productivity” as separate categories are splitting a single human need in half. The feeling is what gets you opened; the plan is what gets you renewed.

This is the one idea that transfers to every vertical below.

Voice, not text, is the format

Over 70% of interactions are spoken. That changes the relationship — you talk to it while cooking, driving, folding laundry, which is why sessions run 30–40 minutes instead of three. The engineering constraint is brutal and specific: the full loop has to stay inside about two seconds or immersion collapses. Shaving 0.7s off speech initiation was a headline result.

Sub-second responsiveness decides whether it feels conversational or mechanical.

The paywall went up in week three — and that helped

Inference costs on 30-minute daily voice sessions forced monetisation almost immediately. Mehta calls this “a real help to both our growth and product development.” Charging before you are ready removes the option of hiding behind engagement metrics. Industry data backs the structure: hard-paywall apps see roughly 8–9× the revenue per install of freemium apps at day 14 ($2.32 vs $0.27 median), while one-year subscriber retention is effectively identical (27% vs 28%).

You give up almost nothing in retention and gain an order of magnitude in revenue per install.

Character craft is a staffed function, not a prompt

The head of story is a published science-fiction novelist. Animators shaped the creature. Behavioural researchers, writers and game designers review conversation logs, curate datasets, tune prompts in a playground and ship to production without an engineering handoff. That is an org-design decision, and it produced a 4× improvement in prompt-iteration velocity.

Personality is a content pipeline with non-engineers in the loop.
The eighth, quieter one: category arbitrage

Tolan does not list itself under Social or Entertainment, where it would be buried under Instagram and Netflix. It sits in Health & Fitness — a category where a top-20 position is achievable and where the audience is already primed to pay a subscription for feeling better. Pick the App Store category you can win, not the one that describes you most literally.

03 — Distribution

The growth engine, in the order they built it

The sequence matters more than any individual tactic. They did not build a marketing plan and execute it — they found a hook by accident, verified it organically for free, and only then paid to reproduce it.

Step 1

Find the hook for free

An unpaid clip of a real person venting to their alien outperformed anything planned. That emotional situation — not the feature list — became the creative brief.

Step 2

Flood at volume

Many creator accounts, several posts a week each, cheap per video. Comparable operators run 40+ accounts and 200+ videos a week; roughly 2–3% clear 100k views and under five videos drive ~80% of all views.

Step 3

Ride the spikes

When a video hit, installs ran 10–15k in a day and effective cost per install collapsed toward zero. The algorithm, not a following, did the targeting.

Step 4

Industrialise it as paid

Paid creative restages the organic hook with cast actors rather than reusing raw screen recordings — the same emotional scene, produced reliably, bought at scale.

Two details in that sequence are easy to miss and expensive to get wrong. First, the winning hooks are humour and relatable situations with a face on camera — the app is often not even shown, just named in the caption. Product demos do not travel. Second, they did not buy media until organic had proven which scene converts, which is why their cost per install was low enough to survive a $10/month price point.

The finding that should change your plan

Tolan went viral among Gen Z women on TikTok. The people who actually pay are women aged 35–45, many of them mothers in the American Midwest, who found it through cooking content rather than tech media. Mehta’s words: “We didn’t set out to build for Midwestern moms… Midwestern moms showed up.”

Your distribution audience and your monetisation audience do not have to be the same people, and forcing them to be will cost you one of the two. Build the creative for whoever shares; build the pricing, the onboarding and the roadmap for whoever pays. Then go look at who is actually paying you, because it is probably not who you designed for.

04 — Risk

What is cracking — and why that is your opening

Copy the engine, not the trajectory. Tolan in mid-2026 is a company whose growth has stalled, and the reasons are structural rather than execution failures.

  • Novelty decays and there is nothing underneath it. A user cannot tell you whether their Tolan worked. There is no score, no dollar saved, no outcome. Charm has to be re-earned every single month, forever.
  • The category churns hard. AI-powered apps generate roughly 41% more revenue per user but churn about 36% faster than non-AI apps. High ARPU on a leaky bucket is a treadmill.
  • Price fatigue. Complaints about pricing and ads track alongside the estimated traffic decline. A weekly price point extracts well and resents well.
  • The category is now crowded. 337 revenue-generating companion apps exist, 128 of them launched in 2025 alone — and the top 10% capture 89% of the revenue.
  • Regulation arrived. California’s SB 243 took effect 1 January 2026: mandatory disclosure that responses are AI-generated, break reminders every three hours for minors, crisis-response protocols, no sexual content for minors, and a private right of action at the greater of actual damages or $1,000 per violation.
  • Still iOS-only, which in most of the world means most of the market.
The strategic read

Tolan’s ceiling is that it is only a relationship. Your three products each have something it does not: a verifiable outcome. A score that went up. Rent that got collected. A prescription that did not get missed.

So the move is not to build a companion. It is to take the companion’s front end — character, memory, ritual, voice — and bolt it onto a product that can prove it worked. Tolan has to keep being charming forever. An SAT app only has to raise a score once, and the proof does the selling from then on.

05 — The model

Four parts, and you should build all four

Strip out the aliens and the company-specific detail, and what is left is a four-part structure. Tolan has the first three. The fourth is the one you can add.

Part 1

A character with a face

Non-human, customisable, named by the user. It is what gets screenshotted, and it is what makes a subscription feel like keeping a friend rather than renting software.

Part 2

A memory that proves itself

Not perfect recall — selective recall, surfaced unprompted. “You mentioned this last Tuesday.” This is the activation moment. Instrument it and measure the miss rate.

Part 3

A ritual it owns

It opens the loop, daily or weekly, at a time it chose. Never a blank input box. Add a sink for the engagement — Tolan converts talk-time into energy you spend decorating a planet.

Part 4

A proof it delivers

Tolan does not have this. A number that moved because the product existed. It is what survives novelty decay, what justifies a higher price, and what makes the shareable post write itself.

Three operating rules that come with it

  • Charge in week three, not month nine. With AI inference costs, free users are a real liability, and an early paywall forces the product to be worth something immediately. Put it right after the character pairing, when attachment is highest and before the first real interaction.
  • Find the hook before you buy the media. Post 30–50 organic videos across several accounts first. If nothing clears 100k views, you do not have a hook and paid spend will only make that expensive. When one lands, restage it as paid creative.
  • Staff the character. A writer and a designer with production access to prompts, reviewing real conversation logs weekly. This is a hire, not a sprint task. It is what produced the 4× iteration velocity and the retention lift.
06 — Adaptation

Three blueprints

Same four-part model, three very different physics. The SAT app is the closest analogue and should be built first. Property management needs the proactivity but must drop the cuteness entirely.

Blueprint A · highest fit

SAT prep

Teenagers, high anxiety, daily habit required, and an outcome that is a literal number. This is the vertical where the Tolan model is stronger than Tolan.

The one feature
Test anxiety and a study plan are the same feature. “I’m panicking about December” returns reassurance, a six-week plan, and tonight’s twenty questions.
Character
A creature that visibly grows as the score band rises. Not an owl, not a robot — those are taken and generic.
Memory
“That’s the third geometry-angle question this week — same as last Tuesday. Want to kill it tonight?”
Ritual
A 15-minute nightly session it starts, with every wrong answer explained aloud in voice.
Proof
A predicted score band, updated weekly. This is also your viral object — “1180 → 1340” is the post.
Money
Hard paywall after diagnostic + pairing. Parents pay, students use — the same two-audience split Tolan hit by accident. Sell the parent a weekly progress report.
Watch out
Education has the flattest pricing curve of any category and is discounting harder than any other (14.3% of transactions, up from 8.0%). Do not compete on price — monetise the outcome. And SB 243 applies: minors, disclosure, break reminders, crisis protocols, from day one.
Blueprint B · different physics

Property management

Low frequency, high stakes, and the emotion is dread rather than loneliness. Take the proactivity and the memory; leave the alien behind.

The one feature
Landlord dread and admin are the same feature. “Unit 3 hasn’t paid” comes back with the reminder already drafted, the notice period for that state, and the ledger attached.
Character
Competent, not cute. A calm operator with a consistent voice and a name. Cartoon warmth reads as unserious to someone holding a mortgage.
Memory
“This tenant paid on the 9th last April too — send the softer reminder, like last time?”
Ritual
A Monday 07:00 voice briefing: what’s due, what’s late, what’s expiring, what’s broken. Weekly, not daily — match the real cadence.
Proof
Dollarised. “Collected $14,200, six days faster than last month, four hours of your time saved.”
Money
Per-unit, not per-seat. Paywall lands after the first briefing that shows real money, not before.
Watch out
Do not run the TikTok volume play here. This audience lives in landlord forums, Facebook groups and YouTube. The shareable object is the outcome screenshot and the referral, not the character.
Blueprint C · two characters

AushadiExpress

A pharmacy marketplace has two distinct users and needs two distinct companions — and the daily loop is already there, in the dosing schedule.

The one feature
Adherence worry and refill logistics are the same feature. Consumer side: the dose check-in. Retailer side: the stock check-in.
Character
Two. A warm health companion for the customer; a sharp shop-floor operator for the retailer. Same engine, different persona card and tone guidance.
Memory
“Your BP strips run out Thursday — the shop 400m away has them in stock.” Retailer side: “You ran out of this brand twice last month.”
Ritual
A ten-second voice dose check-in. This is the strongest retention loop of the three because it is genuinely daily and medically motivated — you are not inventing a reason to open the app.
Proof
Adherence streak, doses not missed, money saved against MRP. Retailer: stockouts avoided, faster fulfilment.
Money
Consumer subscription is a hard sell in this market; monetise the retailer side and let the consumer companion drive order volume.
Watch out
Regulated health advice. Copy Tolan’s guardrail architecture exactly: no diagnosis, escalate to a real professional, log every refusal. The guardrails are a trust asset, not a tax.
07 — Execution

Ninety days, in order

This sequence is deliberately hook-first. The most common way to lose money on this playbook is to spend eight weeks building a beautiful character and then discover nothing about it travels.

Wk 1–3

Find the hook before you build anything

Five to ten fresh accounts. Thirty to fifty videos. Faces on camera, humour, a real situation — the panic before a test, the tenant who won’t pay, the prescription you forgot. Product barely visible; named in the caption.

Gate: at least one video clears 100k views. If none does, you do not have a hook yet — iterate the scene, not the app.
Wk 4–6

Character, memory, one ritual, paywall

Ship the pairing onboarding as a real personality quiz — it is simultaneously the personalisation data, the attachment moment and the shareable artefact. Build memory as retrieval, not transcript. Pick exactly one ritual and make the product open it. Hard paywall immediately after pairing, before the first real interaction.

Gate: a user who returns on day 2 is greeted by something they said on day 1.
Wk 7–9

Instrument the character

Give a writer and a designer production access to prompts and a log-review habit. Track memory-miss rate as a first-class metric alongside D1. Get voice latency inside two seconds end to end — if you cannot, ship text-first rather than shipping slow voice.

Gate: a non-engineer ships a persona change to production without a code deploy.
Wk 10–13

Industrialise the winning hook

Restage the organic winner as produced paid creative — cast it, do not screen-record it. Scale the account count. Simultaneously start reading who is actually paying you, and be prepared for it to be a different person than you built for.

Gate: paid CAC below blended 90-day ARPU, and you can name your paying cohort in one sentence.

08 — Corroboration

The model is not a one-off

One data point is a story. Two is a pattern — and the second one contradicts Tolan on monetisation in a way worth knowing.

Finch, a self-care app built around a virtual pet you care for by completing real-life tasks, reached an estimated $30–40M ARR bootstrapped, with over $1M/month on Android alone. Same structure: onboarding starts with hatching the pet rather than a feature tour, the creature’s wellbeing depends on your behaviour, and the emotional bond does the retention work. As one analysis put it: people will journal for their Finch when they would not journal for themselves.

But Finch runs a soft paywall and generous free tier, and got further than Tolan on less capital. The reconciliation is inference cost: Finch’s loop is cheap to serve, Tolan’s 30-minute voice sessions are not. Your paywall hardness should be a function of your marginal cost per active user, not a philosophy. If your product is voice-heavy and token-hungry, gate early like Tolan. If your loop is mostly local and cheap, a great free experience will out-convert a locked one.

The third reference point is Duolingo, which proves the character and the streak can carry a brand to 116M monthly actives — and that the mascot works best when it has an actual personality with edges, not a smiling neutral helper.
